Running simulations

This chapter covers what happens between pressing Run and reading a result: how Aerolume sets up a Navier–Stokes run, what the quality presets mean, how the Reynolds number is handled, when a run stops, and how far to trust the numbers. Sections 1-9 describe 2D runs; 3D CFD (imported meshes) is section 10 and finite-wing analysis section 11 -- they follow the same rules wherever the text does not say otherwise. Section 13 covers what a CFD run can carry besides the flow: heat transfer and buoyancy, heated bodies and heaters, smoke and tracer particles, fans, jets and closed rooms, the solver diagnostics, and editing all of it live in explore mode. The theory behind the solvers is in theory_ns2d.md and theory_ns3d.md; the airfoil polar solver is described in theory_airfoil.md, the wing solver in theory_wing.md.

For an airfoil at Re above about 10⁴ the Polar analysis gives the better drag and lift: it models the boundary layer and transition, which a 2D grid at that Reynolds number cannot resolve. CFD is the right tool when the flow separates, when there is more than one body, or when the ground matters.

2. What the planner decides#

Every CFD run is planned automatically from the design and its simulation settings. The plan is shown in Setup → Estimate (the table is SimPlan.summary(), the lines under it are the planner's notes and warnings), and again at the top of the run log. The planner decides, in this order:

  1. Wind frame. Flow always comes from the left. The angle of attack (or a vehicle's pitch) rotates the geometry about the moment reference point, nose up for positive angles. On a road the wheels stay on the road and only the body pitches; a pitch that would push the body into the road is refused with a message.

  2. Domain. Its size depends on the kind of design:

    designupstreamdownstreamheight

    vehicle on a road1.25–2 lengths3–5 lengthsvehicle height above the road ÷ domain height ≤ 8 % (Draft) or 5 % (Standard, Fine)
    vehicle in free airas aboveas abovefrontal height ÷ domain height ≤ 8 % / 5 %

    Blockage matters more in 2D than in a wind tunnel: the air cannot escape around the sides, so a low ceiling speeds up the flow over the body and raises both drag and lift. A 4.7 m sedan therefore sits under a 17 m (Draft) or 27 m (Standard) ceiling. The grid is uniform, so those cells cost the same as the ones near the car; that is the price of honest blockage. Bluff bodies get long domains because they are cheap and it matters: the Draft cylinder at Re 100 read Cd 1.53 with the inlet 4 diameters upstream and 1.43 with it 10 diameters upstream.

  3. Resolution. Each quality has a preset: cells per chord for airfoils, cells per vehicle height for vehicles, cells per frontal size for other bodies. It is then raised until thin features get enough cells (the airfoil thickness, a car's ground clearance: 4 cells at Draft, 6 at Standard, 8 at Fine), and finally fitted into a cell budget for the device by first shortening the domain upstream and downstream (never below the blockage limit) and only then coarsening the grid. Grid sizes are rounded up to numbers the pressure solver's FFTs handle well.

  4. Reynolds number and flow model (section 4).

  5. Duration and averaging window (section 5).

  6. Run-time estimate: the number of time steps (the duration over the stability time step, which depends on how fast the flow around this kind of shape gets) times the solver's speed on a grid of this size, plus the cost of the live view (about a fifth of the time in the application; the CLI shows the estimate without it) and of building the solver and collecting the outputs. Every run measures all three on this machine and the next estimates use the measurements made on grids of a similar size and at a similar resolution, so a small airfoil run does not set the estimate for a large car grid. The application and aerolume-cli share the measurements in run_time_calibration.json in the user configuration folder (%APPDATA%\Aerolume on Windows), and aerolume-cli bench adds to it too (set AEROLUME_CALIBRATION=off to keep an app or CLI session from using it). Explore sessions never record. Scripts and other programs opt in with aerolume.simulation.use_calibration_file(path). With placed physics the time step is sized for the fastest air the run should reach, not for the reference speed (section 13.7), and once a run has timed its first blocks the Remaining time and plan.est_seconds follow the measured rate (plan.est_seconds_planned keeps the planner's own number).

The budgets are fixed per device class and do not depend on measured speed, so the same design always gets the same grid; comparisons between designs rely on that.

New simulations start at Draft. A simulation created from Setup, from the project tree or with a new study starts at Draft quality, so the first press of Run comes back in seconds to about a minute on a mid-range GPU. Standard and Fine are one click away in Setup; the estimate next to Run shows what they cost before you start. Airfoils above Re 10⁵ also start in the capped Reynolds mode, at Re 2,500 (section 4). A simulation added to a design that has none copies the fluid, speed, angle, quality and Reynolds settings of the study's latest CFD run (a design with another chord gets the Reynolds mode that suits its own Reynolds number), so a second design is compared at the conditions the study already uses.

That is why vehicles keep a 0.4 M-cell Draft budget even though it costs them about 75 s.

On a CPU, vehicles are slow (Draft sedan: about 8 minutes) and the budget forces a coarser grid; the plan says so in its warnings.

4. Reynolds number: real or capped#

Re = ρ U L / μ with L the chord (airfoils) or the vehicle length. Two modes:

  • Real (the default, except for new airfoil simulations above Re 10⁵): the simulation runs at the real Reynolds number. When the grid resolves the laminar boundary layer (at least 4 cells across its thickness at the trailing edge) the flow is computed without a turbulence model (laminar, a direct simulation of the 2D flow). When it does not — a car at 30 m/s has Re ≈ 10⁷ and a boundary layer about 0.2 cells thick — the planner switches to a large-eddy simulation: the WALE sub-grid model adds dissipation where the grid cannot follow the smallest eddies, and third-order upwind advection keeps the run stable. There is no wall model and no transition model, so friction drag and anything that depends on the exact separation point of a thin boundary layer are not quantitative.

  • Capped: the simulation runs at the cap (or at the real Reynolds number when that is lower), as if the fluid were that many times more viscous. Flow and coefficients are those of the capped flow; forces are scaled with the real speed and density. Use it for resolved, laminar flow pictures and for studies where you want every run on the same physics regardless of speed. Heat transfer follows the same rule: the Nusselt number is the capped flow's (its Péclet number is Re_cap × Pr), and heat rates are that Nusselt number with the real conductivity, Q = Nu k ΔT P / L. (Until the round-5 fix a capped 2D run reported the more conductive simulated fluid's heat rate, Re_real/Re_cap times too large: 1814 instead of 34.15 W/m for a NACA 0012 at Re 1.3 × 10⁵ capped at 2,500.) The heat balance stays the simulated flow's own, so it closes; its body and wall terms are Re_real/Re_cap times the heat rates, and the result's notes say so.

    The cap belongs to the simulation and is set when the simulation is made: Re 2,500 for an airfoil, the highest Reynolds number that every quality resolves on every device (Draft on a CPU has 40 cells per chord: (5 × 40 / 4)² = 2,500). So stepping from Draft to Standard refines the grid of the same flow, and a CPU and a GPU simulate the same case. (A cap left to the grid — Auto in Setup, or projects saved before the cap was stored — followed the grid: the same NACA 2412 case simulated Re 6,400 at Draft and 25,600 at Standard on a GPU and 2,500 on a CPU, so a "grid study" compared different flows. Opening such a project pins the cap to the Reynolds number of its stored result, and a plan without a cap says that its Reynolds number now depends on the grid.) A cap above what the grid resolves gets the large-eddy model and a warning.

Why airfoils above Re 10⁵ start capped: no grid this tool can afford resolves an airfoil boundary layer at Re 10⁶, and without a wall or transition model the "real" run is not closer to reality — NACA 2412 at Re 10⁶ and 4° read Cl 0.16 and Cd 0.097 at Draft, where the viscous polar (and the wind tunnel) give about 0.72 and 0.007. A capped run is at least a resolved, repeatable flow that says what it is.

The polar cross-check. For a single airfoil at Re 5 × 10⁴ or more, every CFD result is also analysed with the viscous polar solver at the same real Reynolds number and angle (about 2 s at the end of the run). When the two disagree (Cl by more than 20 %, Cd by more than a factor 1.5) the result's first warning puts them side by side — "Viscous polar at Re 1.00×10⁶, α 4°, gives Cl 0.718, Cd 0.00697 (this CFD run: Cl 0.150, Cd 0.106)" — and says which to quote. When they agree it is a note.

The Setup table shows both numbers ("Real Reynolds number", "Simulated Reynolds number … (capped)") and the result keeps them.

The flow model can be forced in Setup (Laminar or LES); the planner warns when laminar flow is forced on a grid that cannot resolve it.

5. When a run stops, and what "converged" means#

A run has two phases. Developing the flow lets the start-up transient (the impulsive start, the first vortices) wash out. Averaging accumulates mean fields and the force statistics. About every 1.5 seconds the runner judges the force history:

  • The averaging window starts where the start-up transient really ends, found from the data (MSER-5 on Cd and Cl), never before the planned averaging start.
  • The mean of Cd must be known to the quality's target: its standard error — from batch means over whole shedding periods or integral time scales, so autocorrelation is accounted for — must be below 5 / 3 / 2 % of the mean. Cl must be known to twice that relative precision, or to the same absolute precision as Cd when the body makes no lift.
  • The two halves of the window must agree: a difference that is larger than the target and larger than three standard errors is a drift, not noise.
  • The fluctuation amplitude must have settled. In a laminar flow (deterministic) that means within ±25 % between the halves; a large-eddy simulation is chaotic and its rms has its own sampling error, so there the change must also exceed three standard errors. Two refinements keep this rule honest at both ends. A fluctuation that is decaying and whose peak (√2 × rms) fits inside the target cannot move the mean by more than the target, so its standard error alone decides and it does not count as shedding: the heated cylinder at Re 40 (steady, below Re ≈ 47) used to end "not converged" over a start-up wiggle on its lift of rms 0.0099 → 0.0063 against a ±0.083 target. A growing fluctuation is never waved through, however small, because that is how vortex shedding sets in.
  • A periodic flow must have at least four averaged cycles.

When all of this holds, the run stops early and the result is marked converged. If the drag is still drifting early in the window, the field averages restart once and the run is extended by up to 50 % (not when you set the duration yourself). A run that reaches its end without meeting the target is reported as not converged with the uncertainty it did reach. When the window held too few independent samples to measure that uncertainty (fewer than about 20, or under 50 for noise-like signals), the warning says the error bars are rough estimates: they are then a deliberately cautious figure (it also covers start-up transient left in the window), the report marks them with ~ and Compare does not call a difference significant on them. A Draft NACA 4412 at Re 1 000 stopped at 20 L/U used to be reported as "usable with the error bars shown" while its means were 4.2 of those error bars from the converged values.

What to expect: laminar flows (low-Re airfoils, the cylinder) settle quickly and usually stop early. 2D large-eddy simulations of cars do not: the Draft sedan's drag fluctuates by ±40 % of its mean with an integral time scale of about 0.26 L/U, and big vortex events modulate the lift for the first 15 convective times. Its mean drag is known to about ±3–8 % after 30 L/U, so a Draft car often ends just short of its ±5 % target and is reported as not converged — with its error bars. Getting to ±1 % would need several hundred L/U; set a longer Duration if a comparison needs it.

The lift target is twice the drag target (±10 / 6 / 4 %): in separated 2D flows the lift fluctuates far more than the drag, and the early stop is about the drag. When the mean lift is near zero (a symmetric body), the target is the absolute floor tied to the drag instead, and the result says so: "targets ±5 % and ±0.083 (absolute: the mean is near zero)" rather than a relative error of a zero mean.

Stopping a run. Stop (or Ctrl+C in the command line) ends the run at the next block of steps. The partial result is kept and saved, with a warning that it is averaged over a shorter window (or, if averaging had not started, that it is a rough indication from the last half of the record). A stopped Run again of a simulation that already had a complete result keeps that result instead (the same when the window closes or another project opens during the re-run). If the project folder refuses to save a result (OneDrive or a virus scanner holding the file, a full disk), the result is still shown, marked not saved yet, and saved again in the background; Simulation ▸ Save unsaved results tries again at once, and quitting asks first.

6. Reading results honestly#

  • Error bars. Every mean is shown with its standard error. Two designs differ meaningfully when their difference is larger than about twice the combined error.
  • 2D is not 3D. A 2D vehicle is an infinitely wide side profile. Its drag and lift coefficients (per frontal height) are well above wind-tunnel values for the same car: the sedan template gives Cd ≈ 0.6 and Cl ≈ 2.8 at Draft (0.72 and 2.2 at Standard), where a real saloon measures about 0.3 and 0.1–0.4. The wake sheds as a regular 2D vortex street, and the underbody is a channel rather than a gap between four wheels. Rank and compare designs simulated the same way; do not quote the numbers as a car's Cd.
  • Wheels. In 2D a wheel is an infinitely long cylinder. A wheel touching the road seals the underbody completely and makes the lift meaningless; the planner warns. Leave wheels off, or lift them by a small wheel gap. On a moving road, rotating wheels roll with the belt (ω = U / r, counter-clockwise for flow from the left).
  • Ground clearance. At Draft the gap under a car is 4 cells: air passes (on a moving road the Draft sedan's underbody air moves at about 0.9 U on average), but half of those cells are the diffuse wall, so the flow under the car and the suction that sets the lift are under-resolved. Below about 8 cells (Fine) the plan and the result lead with "Underbody gap under-resolved": compare lift between designs run at the same quality, and use Fine before quoting it.
  • Blockage. Shown in the Setup table. Above the preset's limit (only possible with a hand-set domain height) drag and lift read high; the plan warns.
  • Sharp edges. Bodies are immersed in a uniform grid through a wall about one cell thick, and sharp corners converge more slowly than rounded shapes: the square cylinder at Re 100 reads Cd 5.9 % high at 32 cells per side and 3.7 % at 48, while its Strouhal number is right at both (validation report, section 3). Compare sharp-edged designs at the same quality.
  • Strouhal number is reported when the lift oscillates with a clear dominant frequency, based on the chord (airfoils) or the frontal height (vehicles, bodies). In a chaotic LES wake it describes the dominant shedding and varies between runs. It is shown as "—" when there is nothing to measure: the oscillation is negligible (under 2 % of the lift or drag) or has no clear spectral line, fewer than four cycles were averaged, or the run is an airfoil whose boundary layers the grid does not resolve (the "Lift and drag are not reliable" warning). In that last case the lift does oscillate -- a NACA 2412 at Re 10⁶ and 4° at Draft rings at a chord-based 1.66 -- but the oscillation belongs to the under-resolved flow, not to the attached airfoil; the result's notes say which reason applied.
  • Surface cp and cf come from probes 1.5 and 2.5 cells off the wall. cp is reliable wherever the flow is resolved; cf is a lower bound when the boundary layer is thinner than about three cells (every high-Re run), so read its sign (separation, reattachment), not its size. Forces never come from these curves; they come from the solver's momentum balance.
  • Per-body coefficients (several bodies) are means of samples taken through the averaging window; the totals use every time step.
  • Sensitivity map (Setup → Compute sensitivity): after the run, a reverse sweep through a short window of the developed flow gives dJ/dn for J = Cd, Cl, L/D or Cm on every surface point. Positive values: pushing the surface outward there increases J. In an unsteady flow it is the sensitivity of a short time average; read it as a direction, not a prediction. If the map cannot be computed (memory, a stopped run), the forces are kept and a warning says why.

7. Explore mode#

Simulate → Explore runs a smaller grid in real time while you change the angle, the speed, the road and the displayed field. It covers every pitch the slider can reach (±25°, ±5° on a road, where the pitch is also limited so the body stays above the road), allows up to 10 % blockage and uses float32. Speed changes only change the Reynolds number, so the flow keeps developing instead of restarting; switching the road between moving and fixed rebuilds the solver with the current flow. Explore results are for looking, not for numbers: use Run for those. With placed physics explore also moves emitters, fans, heaters and jets, draws obstacles, holds the Reynolds number while ν or U changes, pauses and steps, and captures a solver step (section 13.8).

10. 3D CFD#

A design is 3D when it was made from meshes (Geometry ▸ Import STL / OBJ / PLY, or aerolume-cli quick3d) or from a wing planform. The same planner, runner and live view handle it; only the geometry, the domain and the outputs change. Frame (as in a wind tunnel): x downstream, y to the left, z up, the road at z = 0.

10.1 What the planner decides#

  1. Attitude. Yaw turns the body about the vertical axis through the moment reference (positive yaw: the wind comes from the vehicle's left), then pitch turns it nose up. On a road the wheels only yaw; a pitch that pushes the body into the road is refused.

  2. Symmetry plane. A design whose mirror image across y = 0 matches it (measured at import, and again by the planner) is simulated in the half domain y ≥ 0 at zero yaw: half the cells, the same answer (measured on a sphere at Re 100 on the same grid: Cd 1.1562851 with the plane, 1.1562861 without, in 15.5 s instead of 32.6 s). Forces and moments are reported for the whole body; side force, roll and yaw are zero by construction, and wake motions that break the symmetry are suppressed. Yaw, a design that is not symmetric, or Setup ▸ Symmetry ▸ Full simulates both halves.

  3. Domain. Vehicles get 1–1.5 lengths of road ahead and 2–3 behind, bluff bodies 5–6 frontal sizes ahead and 10–12 behind, wings 3–5 mean chords ahead and 5–8 behind. The cross-section keeps the same clearance above and beside the body, sized so that the frontal area is at most a set share of the tunnel cross-section (blockage) and the body fills at most a set share of the tunnel's width and of its height:

  4. Resolution and budget. Cells per vehicle height (24 / 32 / 40 on a GPU), per frontal size of a bluff body (20 / 24 / 32) or per mean chord of a wing (16 / 24 / 32), raised (by at most 1.5×, 1.25× for wings) so a thin feature -- the ground clearance, a wing's thickness, a thin part -- spans 3 / 4 / 6 cells, then fitted into the cell budget by first shortening the domain ahead and behind (bluff bodies and wings may also narrow it, up to 1.6 times the blockage limit, with a warning) and only then coarsening:

    The grid is uniform, so cells far from the body cost as much as those next to it; that is why the budgets bind and why a CPU is practical for Draft only (its grids are coarse: about 9 cells per diameter for a sphere). A grid that would not fit the device's memory is refused with a message.

  5. Reynolds number and flow model: the 2D rules (section 4). A car at 30 m/s has Re ≈ 10⁷: the boundary layers are far thinner than a cell, so the run is a wall-unresolved large-eddy simulation (WALE) with no wall or transition model.

  6. Duration: vehicles 20 / 25 / 30 L/U (averaging from 5 / 6 / 8), bluff bodies 40 / 60 / 80 frontal sizes / U (from half of that), wings 20 / 25 / 30 L/U, with the 2D early-stop rules (section 5); the estimate uses throughput measured for the 3D solver on the reference GPU and is corrected by every run on this machine.

10.3 What a 3D result holds#

  • Six coefficients with error bars: CD (drag), CS (side force), CL (lift, negative = downforce), C_roll, Cm (pitch, + nose up) and Cn (yaw), on the reference area (frontal area for vehicles and bodies, planform area for wings), the reference length for pitch and the span / width for roll and yaw, moments about the moment reference; plus forces [N] and moments [N m].
  • Surface cp, cf and the wall-shear vector at every vertex of your mesh (vertex order kept, so exports and the 3D view line up with the imported file). With a symmetry plane the vertices of the missing half take their mirror image's values. As in 2D, cp is usable, cf a lower bound wherever the boundary layer is thinner than a few cells.
  • Three mean slices: the symmetry (or centre) plane, a horizontal plane at mid-height and a cross-flow plane just behind the body, mirrored to the full width.
  • A flow volume (u, v, w, cp, Q-criterion and the solid fraction, at most 2 M cells, block-averaged from the solver grid when it is larger) for streamlines and vortex iso-surfaces in the 3D viewer.
  • Per-part coefficients when the design has several parts. With a symmetry plane a part that lies wholly on one side and its mirror image share their force half and half.
  • Sensitivity map (Setup ▸ Compute sensitivity): dJ/dn per vertex, on grids up to the sensitivity module's budget (2.1 M cells on a GPU, 0.4 M on a CPU); larger grids skip it with a warning and keep the forces.

The live view during a run, and Explore, show a slice (Simulate ▸ Slice: x, y or z and its position) with the mesh's cross-section drawn on it.

Explore plans a small full-domain grid (about 0.4 M cells on a GPU: yaw needs both halves) that covers pitch ±10° (±3° on a road) and yaw ±15°; changing either rebuilds the solid masks in place (measured: one 0.3 s frame for the Ahmed-type body) and the flow keeps developing. 11. Finite wings#

A wing design is a planform: sections (span station, chord, leading-edge position, twist, airfoil) from the root to the tip, mirrored for a symmetric wing. The airfoil is a NACA code or a 2D airfoil design of the same project ("design:<id>", resolved from the open project). A new simulation of a wing starts as a Wing analysis:

  • Vortex lattice (default): ring vortices on the cambered mean surface; any planform, sweep, dihedral and sideslip; induced drag in the Trefftz plane. Its lift is linear.
  • Lifting line (nonlinear, Phillips–Snyder): for straight or moderately swept wings of aspect ratio 4 or more; the section lift curves come from the viscous polars, so it follows them to cl max.
  • Viscous (default): every spanwise strip takes its profile drag from its section's 2D viscous polar at its own Reynolds number (XFOIL-class, Ncrit and Mach from Setup). Those polars are what a first run spends its time on (about 30 s for one airfoil); they are kept for the session, so a second run with the same airfoils takes well under a second (measured: 30 s, then 0.26 s, for a tapered NACA 2412 wing at six angles). Inviscid sweeps take a second or two.

The result has CL, CD = CDi + CDv, Cm, side force, roll and yaw per angle, the span efficiency e and the span loading, plus a stall-onset estimate (the first section to reach its cl max). It is potential flow plus strip theory: no fuselage, no stall beyond what the section polars contain (Finite wings). For the flow itself, the wing can also be run as 3D CFD (lofted to a mesh); at flight Reynolds numbers that CFD cannot resolve a wing's boundary layers, and the plan says to use the Wing analysis for lift and drag.

Where things are. Positions are metres in the design frame. An item placed with frame="body" turns with the geometry when the angle of attack (and in 3D the yaw) changes, about the same moment point; frame="wind" keeps it where it is in the tunnel. The planner applies that rule once, so the Setup preview, the run and explore agree. An item outside the planned domain is left out with a warning (it would act on nothing). In 3D an item off the centre plane y = 0 turns the symmetry plane off (it would mirror the item into a twin), and so do a swirling fan, a fan or jet blowing across the plane, and different thermal conditions on the front and back walls. Items that are their own mirror images stay: a smoke rake along y = 0, a smoke rake or line whose two ends mirror each other across it (a spanwise rake from y = −0.08 to +0.08 m: its nozzles map onto each other, so the solved half carries the nozzles at y ≥ 0 and the mirror shows the rest), a heater, fan or jet centred on it and blowing along it. The solver puts exactly half of each into the solved half and reports fans, jets and heaters for the whole, so a hot sphere with a smoke rake on its centre line gets the same half-domain grid as without the rake. A particle rake must lie in the plane: the solver reflects a particle released at y < 0 back into the solved half, where it would double its mirror nozzle's streak.

13.1 Heat transfer: the scales the planner chooses#

The solver works with θ = (T − T_ambient) / ΔT. The planner picks the scales and states them, with the dimensionless groups, in the Estimate (Setup) and in plan.summary():

  • ΔT, the temperature scale: the spread of every temperature the case sets (the ambient, bodies and walls held at a temperature, hot jets) -- T_hot − T_cold, the definition the cavity benchmarks use -- or, when larger, the rise a heat flux or a heater produces. A heat flux: q L / (k Nu) with Nu from a laminar correlation (forced flow over a plate, Pohlhausen; natural convection off a constant-flux plate, Sparrow & Gregg; at least 1). A heater is a source in the air, not a wall, so its rise is set by the air that crosses it (physics.heater_rise): P / (ρ c_p u A), with u A the flow its own plume carries through it (u_p = (g β P / ρ c_p)^(1/3) for a 2D line source, the point plume's speed at one heater width in 3D) or a tunnel's free stream across it, whichever is larger; in a closed box plus the box's warming over one turnover, P t / (ρ c_p V); never more than q L / (k Nu) of a solid of the heater's size. It is a scale, not a prediction: the run computes the temperatures. With nothing heated it is 1 K and the plan says the temperature will stay uniform.
  • U, the speed scale: the free stream in a tunnel. A closed room has none, so it is the fastest thing that drives the air: a moving floor, a jet, a speed-controlled fan, a thrust fan's momentum-theory disc velocity √(T / 2ρA) (√(Δp / 2ρ) for a pressure fan); failing those, the free-fall velocity √(g β ΔT H) when Ra ≥ 10³ (so Re = √Gr), else κ / H (the box is conduction-dominated).
  • H, the length of Gr and Ra: the height of a closed box heated only at its walls or by heaters (the cavity convention), otherwise the design's reference length.

From these: Re = U L / ν, Pr = μ c_p / k, Pe = Re Pr, Gr = g β ΔT H³ / ν², Ra = Gr Pr, and the solver's buoyancy coefficient Ri = g β ΔT L / U². With a forced flow the Estimate also shows the Richardson number Gr / Re² on H and calls the regime Forced below 0.1, Mixed up to 10 and Natural above; a closed box without forcing is Conduction below Ra 10³ and Natural above. Gravity is along −y in 2D and −z in 3D. The two Richardson numbers differ only in their length, and the plan names it: the solver's coefficient is on L, the Estimate's on H (row "Length of Gr, Ra and Ri"). The 4 × 3 m fan room with a 0.5 m fan reads Ri 0.884 on the reference length 0.5 m and 5.30 on the box height 3 m.

Fluid properties at the ambient temperature: air from Sutherland's law for the conductivity (White, Table 1-3), c_p 1006 J/(kg K), β = 1/T; water from Incropera & DeWitt Table A.6 (0-100 °C). Typed values win. A custom fluid without typed values gets air's as placeholders, and the plan warns: a run never quietly uses air's conductivity for another fluid.

One set of numbers. ΔT, β·ΔT, Gr, Ra and Pe are derived once (simulation.physics.thermal_estimate, the planner's own derivation, cheap enough for the Setup page to call on every change), and every line that quotes them reads that one result.

Checks in the plan. β ΔT above 0.1 warns that the Boussinesq approximation (density constant except in the buoyancy force) loses accuracy, and the line names the ΔT it used; Ra above 10⁸ warns that the thermal boundary layers turn unsteady and then turbulent. A preset fluid made at another temperature than the ambient (ISA sea-level air is 15 °C) is a warning too: the run would take its density and viscosity at one temperature and its conductivity, c_p and β at the other. Set Flow ▸ Fluid to the ambient temperature (al.core.fluid.air(20)) to clear it; simulation.physics.default_ambient(fluid) is the ambient that agrees with a preset fluid (15 °C for ISA sea-level air). A plan holds one warning per quantity (physics.warning_key; physics.merge_warnings merges a page's own lines into the plan's without a second β·ΔT or Ra).

Buoyancy-dominated tunnel runs. Above Ri = Gr / Re² = 10 the plume, not the free stream, sets the speeds, and the force coefficients, normalised by the free stream's q = ½ ρ U², become mostly buoyant force over a small q. The Draft hot sphere (D 0.1 m, Re 100, 60 °C in 20 °C air, Ri 627) read Cd 6.4 and Cl 119 on q = 0.00013 Pa. The plan warns, and the result adds the coefficients on the buoyant dynamic pressure ½ ρ u_b², u_b = √(g β ΔT L) (Cd 0.010 and Cl 0.19 for the sphere: the coefficients times U² / u_b² = 1 / Ri). Compare such runs by their forces in newtons. The tunnel is sized for blockage, not for a rising plume: the sphere's plume reached the frictionless ceiling 0.21 m above it and spread under it, which is where the run's fastest air was; the plan says how far the ceiling is.

Draft runs of buoyancy-dominated cases are shortened. The time step of such a run follows the plume, so the free stream's duration costs u_peak / U times the steps: the Draft hot sphere was planned at 40 L/U, 189,682 steps and about 40 minutes on the RTX 4060 Ti. At Draft the planner now keeps the step count of the same case without its plume, t_end × U / u_peak, within 2 to 10 L/U (physics.draft_buoyant_duration); the hot sphere runs 2 L/U (the plume travels about 49 sphere diameters in that time), and the plan says so before Run, with the estimate for the shortened run. That is enough for pictures, not for converged forces: Standard quality or a Duration typed in Setup runs the full length, and a closed box suits a heated body in nearly still air better, because its time scale is the plume's own.

Planned estimate, then measured. A heater's or heat flux's ΔT is an estimate of the rise. Planned as q L / (k Nu), the Draft fan room with a 300 W/m heater, a supply jet and a vent read ΔT 44.1 K (β·ΔT 0.15, a Boussinesq warning; Ra 1.3 × 10¹¹), and its warmest fluid was 3.31 K above the ambient on the time average (3.97 K at the hottest instant; measured during the review before the 0.1.0 release). The through-flow rule above plans it at 4.1 K, and a 500 W/m room at 5.6 K against 5.6 K measured. When ΔT was estimated, a run still re-states the numbers on what it measured (physics_run.apply_measured_thermal):

  • ΔT becomes the warmest fluid's rise above the ambient (or the coldest fluid's drop below it, whichever is larger; t_fluid_max / t_fluid_min, time-averaged), and Gr, Ra, the Richardson number Gr / Re² and the regime are derived from it (physics.result_scales, which says "measured" or "planned estimate"). U, Re and Pe stay those of the run.
  • The Rayleigh, thermal-resolution and buoyancy-dominated warnings are re-derived on the plan's grid from the measured scales and replace the planned ones. The Boussinesq bound is checked against the largest difference seen at any instant (mean field and last step). A run that exceeds the bound without having been warned gets the warning.
  • The plan's temperature-scale and buoyancy notes stay in the result's notes, prefixed "Planned estimate:", and one note gives ΔT, Gr and Ra measured next to the planned values.

A ΔT the case sets (bodies, walls or jets at fixed temperatures: the cavity's T_hot − T_cold) is held exactly and is not re-stated.

Thermal resolution. The grid is checked against the thermal boundary layer: in a forced flow the laminar momentum layer thinned by Pr^(−1/3) (Pr^(−1/2) below Pr 1); in a naturally convecting box H / (2 Nu) with Nu from the cavity correlation of Berkovsky & Polevikov (Incropera eq. 9.50). When buoyancy outruns the forced flow (Ri = Gr / Re² > 1) the layer is the plume's (physics.buoyant_layer, Bejan's scalings on the length of Ra): L·Ra^(−1/4) for natural convection (Ri > 10) and L·(Re_b·Pr)^(−1/2) with Re_b on the buoyant velocity for mixed convection. Sized on the free stream, the Draft hot sphere (D 0.1 m, Re 100, Ri 590, Ra 4.2 × 10⁶) was told "about 12 cells across the thermal boundary layers"; its layer is about 2 mm, 0.4 of its 5 mm cells, and the plan now says so. Below about 4 cells across the layer the plan warns that heat rates and Nusselt numbers are under-resolved. A case in which no body or wall exchanges heat (a room warmed only by heaters or hot jets) has no such layer and no Nusselt number, and the heat input is imposed, so it gets a note instead of the warning, and its grid is the box's own (64 cells across the smaller side at Draft), not one sized for a boundary layer it does not have.

Unvalidated regimes are named. Natural or mixed convection around a heated or cooled body is not validated (Validation report, §10: the validated heat transfer is laminar natural convection in cavities heated at their walls and forced convection past a cylinder and a sphere). Such a plan and its result carry the warning "… around a heated body: regime not validated (Validation report, §10)". The Draft hot sphere above read Nu 53.5 where the natural-convection correlation of Churchill (1983) gives 22.6 at its Ra and Pr 0.71.

13.2 Heat transfer: what a result holds#

  • Nusselt number (result.nusselt, mean ± standard error over the averaging window). With heated bodies: the mean over them of each body's Nu = Q L / (k (T_wall − T_ambient) A) on the reference length (a heat-flux body uses its mean surface temperature). In a closed box without heated bodies: the Nusselt number of its hottest wall, Nu = (Q / A) H / (k ΔT), the definition of de Vahl Davis (1983) and of the Rayleigh–Bénard literature. A steady value is reported as steady, not "± 0".
  • Heat rate (result.heat_rate): W in 3D, W per metre of span in 2D, from the bodies into the fluid (from the hot wall in a closed box without heated bodies). When nothing is held at a temperature -- a room warmed by a heater, a tunnel with a heat source -- it is the heat the heaters and heat-flux walls put into the fluid, measured from the solver's balance, and there is honestly no Nusselt number (the result's notes say so). Per body: per_body[name]["nu"], ["heat_rate"], ["t_wall"] (°C).
  • Fluid temperatures (result.diagnostics): t_fluid_mean (weighted by the fluid fraction), t_fluid_min, t_fluid_max in °C on the solver's full grid (time-averaged when the run averaged), t_fluid_dev_max (K, the largest difference from the ambient at any recorded instant) and, with heaters, heat_sources_set (their set power, to compare with the measured heat_in_sources).
  • Fields: temperature in °C (the mean and the end-of-run fields; 3D slices and the stored volume too), temperature_rms in K.
  • Heat balance (result.diagnostics): heat in through bodies, walls, heaters, jets and the inlet, out through the outlet and vents, the storage rate, and their residual, in W or W/m. The command line prints the residual as a share of the largest heat flow. When more than 10 % of the heat put in is still being stored at the end (the fluid is still warming up, physics_run.storage_share), the result warns that the temperatures, the heat rate and the Nusselt number were still changing, and the Nusselt number's error bar is marked a rough estimate: a standard error over a window that is still drifting is not the uncertainty of the mean (the Draft hot sphere stored 69 % of its heat and once reported Nu 53.51 ± 0.05).
  • History: nu and heat_rate per step, plotted on the Results page and judged by the convergence verdict (13.6).

13.3 Smoke and particles#

Smoke goes into up to four independent channels by colour: emitters of one colour share a channel, a jet that carries smoke uses the first (default) colour, and colours past the fourth fold into the last channel (the plan warns, naming the colour they are drawn in). The live view and the stored frames composite the channels in their colours over a dark background. Placed smoke replaces the automatic inlet comb (2D) or seed rake (3D). rate is the released concentration (clipped to 0 … 1, with a note), pulses (pulse_period, pulse_duty) and start / stop are seconds of flow time.

Particles are released at rate per second from the emitter's nozzles and carried by the flow (RK2, bilinear / trilinear); a steady emitter draws streaklines, a pulsed one timelines. Frames carry them as points with their emitter's colour and, for point and rake emitters, as streak lines. A result stores the smoke (the end-of-run channels) but not the particles.

13.4 Fans and jets#

A fan is an actuator disc (a strip in 2D) of diameter and thickness (default an eighth of the diameter, at least two cells): "thrust" (N, or N/m in 2D), "pressure_jump" (Pa) or "velocity" (the disc-averaged speed through it in m/s, which the solver holds with a PI controller on the thrust, inside the CUDA graph). swirl (3D) adds a tangential velocity. The plan warns below 8 cells across the disc: the thrust is still delivered, the induced velocity and the flow rate are then indicative. result.fans_report gives, per fan, the delivered thrust (with its standard error), pressure jump, volume flow, disc and induced velocity and ideal power, all in SI.

A jet blows fluid in at speed along direction through an opening of size, with its own temperature (heat transfer on) and smoke; a vent sucks the same flow out. Mass and momentum fluxes are exact. In a closed room the jets must balance the vents: the plan compares the volume flows (2 % tolerance) and warns, because the solver otherwise removes the difference evenly over the whole box to keep the pressure problem solvable. The opening wants at least 4 cells; fewer keep the flow exact and the profile approximate.

13.5 Closed rooms#

With Domain ▸ Closed box the tunnel's inlet and outlet become no-slip walls (a moving floor is a driven cavity). The box is domain_box, or the bodies and placed items with one size of room on every side. The grid fits the walls exactly in height and to the nearest cell in width (2D; FFT-friendly sizes where the shape allows) or in multiples of 4 cells (3D), and has 64 / 96 / 128 cells across the smaller side at Draft / Standard / Fine on a GPU (48 / 64 / 80 on a CPU; 3D: 32 / 48 / 64, CPU 16 / 24 / 32), refined to 4 / 6 / 8 cells across the thermal boundary layer of a naturally convecting box. The run length follows the physics: (45 + 0.225 Pe_H) free-fall times for natural convection (the validation cavities settled in 12-144, so 1.5 times that, capped at 1500), 1.5 diffusion times H²/κ for conduction, and 30 / 40 / 50 box turnovers for fans and jets; the run stops earlier once its monitors settle. That steady-state rule does not fit rooms: from Ra 10⁸ on the flow is unsteady and never reaches the steady state it waits for, and the Draft heater-only 4 × 3 m room was planned at 1500 free-fall times, 9000 L/U and 49 minutes. Draft therefore runs such boxes for 40 free-fall times (the large-scale circulation is established within a few tens), with a note; Standard and Fine, or a Duration typed in Setup, keep the long rule. The Duration note is built from the duration actually used ("6 L/U (1.00 free-fall times H/U, set in Setup, …)" for the heater-only room, which had read "1500 free-fall times"). Coefficients, where bodies exist, use q = ½ ρ U² with the reference velocity. A closed box that nothing drives is refused with a message. A box heated at a set rate with no way for the heat to leave (no vent, no wall or body at a set temperature) warms by P / (ρ c_p V) per second for as long as it runs; the plan says so, with the rate, and its temperatures are not convergence monitors (they never settle).

2D rooms with fans are qualitative. In 2D the fan's power can only leave the room through wall friction and numerical dissipation (2D flow has no vortex stretching, so energy gathers in room-sized vortices), and the room's speeds have no grid-converged answer: for the 1 N/m fan in the 4 × 3 m room the mean speed is 2.4 m/s at 6 and 12 cells per fan width and 3.4 m/s at 24, and the room needs 500-900 L/U to settle against the 240 L/U a Draft run plans (Navier–Stokes solver (2D), §18.4). The thrust is exact and the flow pattern is meaningful, but speeds, the disc velocity, volume flow and convective heat transfer are not results. Every 2D closed domain with a fan therefore carries that warning, first, in the plan and in the result (so the Results banner and the report show it), with a sentence when the planned duration ends inside the start-up transient. Use 3D for rooms: a 3D fan's jet breaks down into 3D turbulence, which dissipates its power inside the room.

13.6 When a run with placed physics stops#

Besides the forces (if there are bodies in a tunnel), the verdict judges monitors: the Nusselt number (to half the quality's tolerance, since the references are quoted to 0.1-1 %; it stands for the bodies' heat rate, or their wall temperature for heat-flux bodies), each fan's disc velocity (the thrust a speed-controlled fan needs), and in a closed box the mean kinetic energy and, when heat goes in at a set rate and can leave, the rise of the warmest and of the mean fluid above the ambient (t_rise_max, t_rise_mean, in K: a rise, so a relative tolerance means something). A steady monitor must also stop drifting: its change between the halves of the averaging window has to be under a tenth of its tolerance, because a value settling exponentially sits within the tolerance of its current value long before it reaches the asymptote. The Estimate's "Stops early when" lists them, and the run log says which monitor held a run back.

A closed box is never judged on its forces. Force coefficients on a box's own reference velocity mean nothing inside it, and the Summary hides them; the Electronics enclosure once ended "Not converged" because its circuit board's Cd was known to only 12 %, with no visible reason on screen. Closed boxes are judged on their monitors alone, and a not-converged message names the one that held the run back and every other one the box is judged on.

Diagnostics (record_diagnostics, on by default): one row every 10 steps with the RMS and maximum divergence, the pressure-solve residual, the L2 / RMS / maximum / relative change of each field, the CFL number, the step and the throughput. Explore streams them to the Diagnostics panel (exportable as CSV); a result keeps the last row and the heat balance. adaptive_dt="pid" replaces the per-step CFL rule by a PID-controlled step (smoother, at most +0.2 % change per step, Navier–Stokes solver (2D), §20).

13.7 Time step and run-time estimate with placed physics#

The time step is set by the fastest air, so the planner estimates the peak speed (simulation.physics.peak_speed) as the largest of the reference velocity, the buoyant velocity √(g β ΔT H) (√Gr times that below Gr 1), twice each fan's disc velocity (the slipstream of momentum theory), the jets and a moving floor, and plans the step with the measured speed factor (max|u| + max|v| (+ max|w|)) relative to it. The coefficients and the Reynolds number keep the reference velocity. Each kind of case has its own measured factor (plan.calibration_kind), refined by every run of that kind:

Closed boxes are calibrated per case signature. How fast a box recirculates depends on what is in it: the plain Draft 2D fan room measured a factor of 5.09 of its peak, the same room with a supply jet, a vent and a 300 W/m heater 3.87 (the jet and the vent bleed the circulation), the electronics enclosure 2.68 (laminar), and a sealed heater-only room 2.35 of its free-fall velocity, where the cavities measured 0.5. One shared "closed-fan" factor let the other rooms' runs move the furnished room (fan, heater, supply jet and vent) from 52 s planned (after its own 49 s run) to 164 s. The key is now the class plus the signature and the grid class: closed-fan+jet+heat@64 (jets, buoyant heat besides the fan), +heater (a natural box warmed by heaters), +body (bodies inside), @n (cells across the smaller side, rounded to a power of two). Each signature starts from the most specific default measured for it (the values above), each run moves its key by at most 1.5 times (plan.clamp_update), and the planner takes the weighted median of the key's runs (plan.speed_median: weighted by resolution and age, with the default as one more voice), so one unusual run cannot swing the next estimate. Explore sessions never record: their obstacles, sliders and placed items change while they run. Measured end to end on the reference machine on 31 October 2026 (validation/records/planner-timing-2026-10-31.json, which also states how): the furnished room ran in 67.2 s on a shared GPU and was then planned at 58.9 s, at 64.9 s after a plain fan room had run and at 65.4 s after the enclosure had (the throughput the other runs measured moves it a little; the speed factor does not).

A heated box at rest. At rest the 2D solver's first step has no bound but the viscous one when nothing sets a source speed (no jet, vent, or speed- or pressure-controlled fan): the sealed Draft heater-only room took a first step of 56 L/U and diverged. Such a run (and an explore session of it) takes its first 10 steps at the planned step (runner.start_from_rest), after which the solver's own buoyant-parcel bound holds; the room then runs its 240 L/U in about 18 s.

Measured effect on the reference machine (RTX 4060 Ti): the Draft enclosure was planned at 28,321 steps and 1 min 05 s and took 121,433 steps and 5 min 06 s on a shared GPU (an earlier development build); it is now planned at about 101,000 steps and 2 min 14 s headless and runs in 2 min 32 s with the GPU otherwise idle (the 2D GPU throughput table was re-measured on an idle GPU on 31 October 2026: 1.4-1.6 times the earlier, shared-GPU rows, which had made first 2D estimates about 1.6 times too long; both measurements and the procedure are in validation/records/planner-timing-2026-10-31.json). Planned before any run, the furnished room reads 74 s headless against 62-67 s measured, the plain fan room 56 s against 51 s. The 3D room (estimated 6 min 31 s, observed about 20 min) is now estimated at 21 min, and the hot sphere's step is planned at the 0.0002 L/U it runs at. Once a run has timed two blocks, Remaining is the measured time: the wall time so far plus the remaining flow time at the measured rate (simulation.runner.refine_estimate).

Cost. The estimate multiplies the time per step by the measured cost of each feature in use (2D, on a shared GPU, so indicative: heat transfer +55 %, buoyancy +9 %, smoke +21 %, particles +19 %, fans +9 %, jets +14 %; 3D, idle GPU: heat transfer +30 %, each smoke channel after the first +6 %, particles +11 %, fans and jets +3 % each). The plan note gives the total; each run feeds its measured throughput back into the estimate.

13.8 Explore with placed physics#

Explore (section 7) edits the placed physics while the flow runs, without a restart:

  • Move and retune emitters, fans, heaters and jets (drag them in the 2D view, or edit them in Setup). The solver rewrites its sources in place and keeps its CUDA graphs when only positions or values change; adding the first fan or a new smoke colour re-captures them once.
  • Draw obstacles (2D): brush strokes and polygons add or erase solid; they are penalised to rest with the bodies' calibrated wall, insulate, clear smoke, and are replayed exactly after an undo, an erase or a road switch. In 3D the session places balls and boxes (post(obstacles={"ops": [{"kind": "sphere", "center": …, "radius": …}]})).
  • The Reynolds lock: hold two of U, ν and Re and edit the third. A new ν changes Re (and Pe, at the same Prandtl number); a new speed re-expresses everything scaled by U (fan thrust, jet speed, heater power, buoyancy), and the SI readouts follow.
  • Pause, step and frame skip: step a given number of steps while paused, or fix the steps per frame; the target frame rate paces the rest.
  • Solver trace: capture one step's stages (advection-diffusion, divergence, pressure solve, correction; in 3D cut on the slice shown) into the next frame.
  • Readouts: frames carry the Nusselt number, the heat rate, the temperature range and each fan's thrust and disc velocity in SI (refreshed twice a second), the new diagnostics rows, and, when an edit could not be carried out, a notice in words (a closed box has no speed slider; an item moved outside the domain).

Two things need a restart, and explore says so: switching heat transfer on (the solver then carries a temperature field it did not allocate) and, in 3D, adding smoke to a session that started without any.

  • The fan rooms are unsteady (one large, slowly wandering vortex), so their monitors never settled within 1.15-1.24 times the planned duration and the runs say "not converged"; the thrust of a thrust fan is exact by construction, and the speed controller holds its target to 0.6 % (2D) and 0.02 % (3D) over the averaging window. The 2D rooms' speeds are qualitative (section 13.5): only the imposed quantities in these rows are results.
  • Every run's divergence stayed below 1.2 × 10⁻⁵ U/L, and the heat balance of the last step closed to 3 × 10⁻⁴ of the heat transferred or better (the cavities to 10⁻⁷).

Estimates. The run-time estimate covers the planned duration; the cavities stopped at about two thirds of it. A plan's first estimate on a new machine comes from the reference throughput table, and each run refines it. With placed physics the refinement reads the real flow speeds from the diagnostics: the time step of a heat-transfer run is often set by the temperature's stability bound rather than the flow, and the earlier rule (the CFL number times the cell over the mean step) took that bound for a fast flow -- 27 times too fast after the Ra 10³ cavity -- and multiplied every later cavity estimate by 2.6 (60 s for a 9 s run). With the fix the Ra 10⁴ cavity is estimated at 15 s after a Ra 10³ run, against 9 s for two thirds of its planned duration. A thrust fan in a closed room is the case the estimate gets wrong first: the circulation it drives is much faster than the momentum- theory reference (max |u| + max |v| reached 12.6 times it), so the first run took 8.6 times its estimate, and the plan says so.

--heated T holds every body at T °C (heat transfer on, buoyancy on unless --no-buoyancy; --ambient sets the air temperature, default 15 °C, the standard sea-level air of every command). --fan X,Y[,Z]:D:VALUE[:MODE[:DIR]] (repeatable) places a fan in the tunnel frame: MODE thrust (default), pressure or velocity, DIR its direction (default +x). --closed X0,X1,Y0,Y1[,Z0,Z1] puts the case in a room. The result then lists the Nusselt number, the heat rate, a table of the fans, and the divergence, pressure residual, CFL number and heat-balance residual of the last step.

13.11 Limits#

  • Boussinesq buoyancy only (constant density elsewhere); no radiation; no conduction inside solids (a body is held at a temperature or releases a flux at its surface).
  • One fluid; smoke and particles are passive (they do not change the flow).
  • 2D results are per metre of span: a heated cylinder in 2D is an infinitely long one.
  • The heated-cylinder Nusselt number reads about 2 % above the published values even on a converged grid, the size of the spread between those references (Navier–Stokes solver (2D) 21.2); the turbine-mode fan's induction is 4-5 % below inviscid momentum theory at Re 500 (attributed to viscosity, not proven).
  • The scales are estimates from the inputs: a heat-flux case's ΔT and a thrust fan's U come from correlations and momentum theory; the run, not the plan, gives the temperatures and speeds. In a closed room a thrust fan can drive the air several times faster than its own disc velocity, so the step shrinks and the run takes longer than the first estimate (the estimate learns from each run).
  • Lattice-Boltzmann solvers and neural-operator surrogates are not in this release.
